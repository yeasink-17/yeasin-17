Conference abstract · ICSS 2027

Marriage, Not Motherhood? Why Adolescent Births Are Rising Again in Bangladesh

Abstract for the Second International Conference on Social Sciences (ICSS 2027), School of Social Sciences, SUST, Sylhet. Track 7: Health, Well-being and Social Work.

Yeasin Khan (corresponding and presenting author), Sayantan Chakraborty, Famous Chakma, Ambika Debnath, Sunzida Bahar Borna, Tamima Saiful. Institute of Health Economics, University of Dhaka.

Adolescent birth rateChild marriageMICSKitagawa decomposition

The puzzle

Bangladesh's adolescent birth rate rose from 83 to 92 births per 1,000 girls aged 15–19 between 2019 and 2025. Over the same period, the share of girls aged 15–19 who were currently married rose from 32.9% to 38.9%, even though the standard child marriage indicator (women aged 20–24 married before 18) fell from 51.4% to 47.2%.

The question

Almost all adolescent births in Bangladesh happen within marriage. So was the rise driven by more girls being married, or by higher fertility among girls who were already married?

Data & methods

Quantitative secondary analysis of published estimates from the nationally representative Bangladesh Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) 2019 and 2025, at national and divisional levels. The sample covers girls aged 15–19 (weighted n = 11,950 in 2019 and 10,962 in 2025). We calculated marital fertility rates, decomposed the change in the birth rate using Kitagawa's method, and tested changes with two-sample z-tests.

Key finding

The rise in adolescent births is largely explained by more adolescent marriage, not by changes in fertility within marriage. Policy should therefore prioritise preventing and delaying child marriage, for example by keeping girls in school and enforcing the Child Marriage Restraint Act 2017, alongside existing family planning services.

Course research project · May 2026

University Education and Employability in Bangladesh: Perceived Skills Mismatch Among DU Students

Labour Economics (HE-208), Institute of Health Economics. Group project of four students, submitted to Professor Sharmeen Mobin Bhuiyan, PhD.

Labour economicsPrimary surveyEmployability

The question

Do University of Dhaka students feel their skills match what the labour market demands? And do faculty, year of study, CGPA, gender, training or internships make a difference?

Approach

A quantitative, cross-sectional survey on Google Forms with 76 valid responses. Students rated 11 skills twice on a 5-point scale: how much employers demand each skill, and their own level. We compared the two using paired t-tests, ANOVA, group t-tests, correlation and multiple linear regression.

Key findings

  • 3.17 vs 2.84. Average perceived demand was higher than self-assessed skill, a statistically significant gap (p < .001).
  • Students who had joined training programmes or internships rated their skills significantly higher.
  • Faculty, year of study, gender and CGPA showed no significant effect.
